## Computational Aspects and Performance of PIML in Building Performance Simulation

### Computational Costs and Time Comparisons
*   **Traditional BPS Models (Physics-based)**: These models, such as EnergyPlus and TRNSYS, generally do not require training but rely on solving forward physics equations. Their computational time is highly dependent on model complexity, including geometric details and meshing resolution, and can increase exponentially, ranging from hours to days or even weeks for complex models like urban-scale energy modeling or fine-grained Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations [1] [2]. CFD simulations, in particular, involve solving high-dimensional sparse matrices which are prohibitively expensive for turbulent multi-physical processes [1].
*   **Data-Driven Models (Pure ML)**: While requiring significant time for training, data-driven models benefit from rapid inference speeds. Training time is influenced by factors such as model structure, dataset size, batch size, and the number of training epochs. However, advancements in GPU technology have significantly reduced training time, and inference is highly efficient [2].
*   **Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) Models**: PIML models generally have inference speeds similar to purely data-driven models but often require longer training times due to their more complex structures, which integrate both data-driven components and physical knowledge, increasing computational demands during training [2].
    *   **Specific Examples**: Shao et al. [3] and Wei et al. [4] demonstrated that PIML models can reduce computational costs by 1-2 orders of magnitude compared to traditional CFD for urban-scale airflow modeling [3] and indoor airflow reconstruction, respectively [4]. Wei et al. also reported a 42% decrease in computing time compared to traditional ANNs for indoor airflow reconstruction [4].

### Accuracy Metrics for ML Algorithms
*   **Overall PIML Performance**: PIML models generally enhance predictive accuracy, generalization ability, and physical consistency by integrating physical principles into machine learning frameworks, addressing the limitations of purely physics-based or data-driven approaches [5].
*   **Specific Examples**: Many studies report improved R², RMSE, and MAE values for PIML models:
    *   **Temperature Prediction**: Drgoňa et al. achieved an MSE of 0.59 K with only 10 days of training data [6]. Di Natale et al. outperformed an RC baseline with an MAE of 0.88 °C for 72-hour predictions (RC: 1.48 °C) and extended this to multi-zone tasks achieving an MAE of 1.17 °C (RC: 1.79 °C; LSTM: 1.27 °C) [6]. Nagarathinam et al. improved LSTM results with a MAPE of 0.4% for single-zone air temperature and 0.2% for wall temperature prediction (LSTM: 5% and 3%, respectively), further reducing errors to ~1% for multizone tasks [6]. Wang and Dong achieved an average MAE of 0.44 °C for temperature and 21 ppm for CO2 predictions [6]. Jiang and Dong attained MAEs of 0.43 °C for single-zone and 0.27 °C-0.39 °C for multizone tasks, with humidity predictions reaching MAEs of 0.0002-0.0005 kg water/kg air [6].
    *   **Load Prediction**: Ma et al. [7] outperformed traditional physics-based models, achieving 40-90% improvements in both MAE and CV-RMSE [7]. Michalakopoulos et al. [8] developed a physics-informed DNN that achieved an R² of 0.87±0.01 and an RMSE of 102.69±7.82 [8].
    *   **Fault Detection**: Li et al. achieved a detection rate of 95.37% with an error rate of 0.73% [9]. Ren et al. improved fault detection rate by 27.2% and significantly reduced false alarm rate by 77.4% [9].

### Specific ML Algorithms Tested/Compared
*   **Neural Networks (NNs)**: A broad category including Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [10], Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [11].
    *   **PINNs**: Used for airflow reconstruction [4], pollutant concentration prediction [12], daylighting simulations [12], and acoustic wave equations [12].
    *   **LSTM**: Often compared with PIML models for temperature prediction [6].
    *   **GNN**: Applied for urban wind field prediction [3] and building thermal dynamics [12].
*   **Gaussian Process (GP)**: Used in physics-guided Gaussian Process (PGGP) models for HVAC system performance prognosis [9].
*   **Decision Tree**: Applied for building infiltration prediction [12].
*   **Ensemble Learning**: Frameworks that combine physics-driven and data-driven components for tasks like HVAC load calculations and predicting residuals [13].

### Training Data Generation
*   **Physics-informed Data Set**: This type of data, also known as 'observational biases,' involves using data that inherently reflects physical principles to bias ML models. It can be developed through experimental design, dataset development based on prior physics knowledge (e.g., mathematical models), combining data from physics-based simulations with measured data, or designing well-structured experimental datasets based on domain expertise [10].
*   **Simulation Data**: Many studies generate training data from physics-based simulations (e.g., CFD) [3] [4].
*   **Real Measured Data**: Long-term observational data is used to capture temporal variations in operations and meteorological conditions [4]. Operational data combined with expert knowledge is also used for fault detection and diagnosis [9].

### Limitations of Black-Box Models and Lack of Physical Consistency
*   **Limited Generalization Ability**: Purely data-driven (black-box) models often suffer from limited generalization ability, making it difficult to infer outputs for unseen conditions [1]. This means they struggle to perform well outside the specific conditions they were trained on [14].
*   **Lack of Physical Consistency**: Data-driven models do not guarantee physical consistency. An accurate predictive model does not necessarily ensure a well-behaved response model, which can lead to significant failures in real-world applications [1]. For example, a regular LSTM model might predict a room temperature decrease with increased cooling input, but fail to capture the correct magnitude or speed of the response, even if the training data is sufficient in quantity [15].
*   **Sensitivity to Data Quality and Quantity**: Black-box models are highly sensitive to the quality and quantity of data; missing, incorrect, or biased data often leads to poor model performance [1].
*   **Lack of Interoperability**: It is challenging to exchange, analyze, and interpret these models due to their black-box nature [1].

In summary, while PIML models offer significant advantages in accuracy and efficiency over traditional methods, they introduce complexities in model development and can sometimes have slightly lower accuracy than purely data-driven models on standard datasets due to constrained solution spaces. However, their ability to ensure physical consistency and generalize to unseen conditions makes them a powerful tool for BPS. The choice of the most appropriate PIML approach depends on the specific modeling objective, available physics knowledge, and data resources.
